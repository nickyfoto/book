# The Brief — 2026-09-17
## Top Story
Amazon has permanently lost customer data, and it is not getting it back. In a status update posted September 15, AWS said it was "unable to restore access to the resources and data" held in the mec1-az2 availability zone of its UAE region, and that the same was true across all three availability zones in Bahrain. The strikes landed on March 1. It took six months and a second attack, on April 1, for the company to conclude that some of what its customers stored there is gone for good.

The wording of the update matters more than the headline. AWS wrote that "the damage to our infrastructure spanned multiple availability zones and exceeded what our regional and multi-AZ services are designed to withstand." That is the company conceding a design boundary, and the boundary is the one every availability-zone diagram in every architecture review quietly assumes away. Multi-AZ has been the standard answer to "what if a data center dies?" for the better part of two decades. It was built for power failures, cooling failures, a backhoe through a fiber bundle, a fire suppression system discharging on the wrong floor. It was not built for a drone with a warhead, and AWS now says so in writing.

The architecture guidance held up better than the infrastructure did, which is why the loss is smaller than it could have been. AWS immediately told customers to migrate workloads to other regions and restore from remote backups, and by the time the IRGC hit a remaining Bahrain structure with missiles on July 24 — damage confirmed independently by satellite imagery — the company could describe most affected customers as having already moved. It had also suspended billing in both regions and, per Forbes, issued roughly $150 million in customer credits after the first round of strikes. Six months of restoration work is still ongoing in the UAE, where two of three zones are being recovered, and AWS says it will report again in early 2027.

The part worth dwelling on is what the credits do and don't cover. A $150 million figure sounds large until you price the thing that actually failed. Credits compensate for a service you didn't receive. They do not resurrect a database. Every team that has ever treated "we're multi-AZ" as a synonym for "we have a backup" just watched that assumption get tested by a state military and lose. The distinction between redundancy and durability — copies in a second zone versus copies somewhere that cannot be hit by the same weapon in the same week — is the one that cost someone real data here. If your disaster recovery plan has a single provider and a single geography in it, this is the week to notice that.

There's a second thing the update establishes, less comfortably: this is now a category, not an incident. The March strike, the April strike, and the July strike were separate operations targeting the same asset over five months, and AWS's response has shifted from "restoring service" to "replacing affected infrastructure" with a timeline measured in quarters. A hyperscaler treating a region as rebuildable rather than recoverable is a different posture than the industry is used to. The war that produced it has since widened — attacks on shipping in the Strait of Hormuz and Bab al-Mandeb have turned it into an energy crisis — so there is no reason to expect the pressure on Gulf-region capacity to ease. The Gulf's AI buildout was sold on cheap power and proximity. Its physical security is now a line item nobody modeled.

The concrete thing to watch is whether AWS publishes anything resembling a revised availability-zone specification — what a zone is now engineered to survive, and what customers should assume it won't. The company has promised a further update in early 2027 and said it remains committed to Bahrain long term. Until that specification exists, every multi-AZ claim in every architecture diagram is an assertion about a threat model that a vendor has not stated. Ask your provider what theirs is. The answer, as of this month, is that it doesn't cover this.

**Migrating the GitHub Copilot runtime to Rust, using Copilot** · [Source](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/generative-ai/migrating-the-github-copilot-runtime-to-rust-using-copilot/)
GitHub rewrote the Copilot agent runtime — the harness behind the CLI, the app, the SDK, VS Code, and half of Office — from TypeScript into more than 800,000 lines of production Rust, shipped across 128 pull requests that landed in main incrementally rather than in one cutover. Most of the code was written by agents; a project GitHub estimates at a year or two of team time took one developer a few months. The stated motive is concrete: every SDK consumer was shipping a Node and V8 runtime per client, roughly 100 MB of working set minimum. The interesting number is the churn underneath: about 430,000 lines of TypeScript passed through the port while the repo's TypeScript count looked flat.

**Bonsai 2 27B: Near-Lossless Compression in a 9x Smaller Footprint** · [Source](https://prismml.com/news/bonsai-2-27b)
PrismML's Ternary Bonsai 2 27B, built on Qwen3.8 27B, uses ternary {−1, 0, +1} weights with FP16 group-wise scaling — 1.76 effective bits per weight, 5.9 GB total footprint, 262K context, Apache 2.0 — and scores 83.9 aggregate against the full-precision model's 85.4, or 98.2 percent retention, up from 95 percent in the previous generation. It runs 143 tokens/second on an RTX 5090 and, on a 4090, 0.714 mWh per token, which PrismML says beats an 8B full-precision model by 40 percent. Vendor-run benchmarks on a vendor's own whitepaper deserve the usual discount, and the "lossless" framing is marketing. But a 27B-class multimodal model that fits in 5.9 GB changes what runs on a laptop.

**Be alert: targeted attacks on prominent Rustaceans** · [Source](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/17/targeted-attacks-on-rustaceans/)
The Rust security team says an ongoing campaign is targeting rust-lang members and owners of popular crates, luring them onto video calls framed as job or contract opportunities, then getting them to install something — a purported missing audio codec — or run a command pasted to the clipboard, in order to publish malware from their accounts. The same trick worked last month against the arrayref crate. This is not a Rust problem so much as the shape of open source: every package in your dependency graph is a person with publish rights and a phone. Cooldowns on new releases buy days of detection, which is cheap insurance and still not standard practice.
