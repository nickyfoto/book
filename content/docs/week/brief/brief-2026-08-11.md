# The Brief — 2026-08-11
## Top Story
The "hidden" reasoning that OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google sell as encrypted is not hidden. A group of researchers has demonstrated a decoding pipeline that reads proprietary reasoning traces from Claude, GPT, and Gemini models in plaintext — and the traces leak real secrets.

Applied to 6,708 publicly available agent trajectories on GitHub and Hugging Face, the pipeline reconstructed 315,320 reasoning blocks and recovered 704 distinct privacy artifacts: 62 API keys, 33 passwords, 24 access tokens, 30 personal email addresses, plus names, postal addresses, and internal URLs. The damning detail is the 64 artifacts that appeared *exclusively inside the reasoning blocks* — nowhere in the visible session. That is not a model flubbing an answer; it is a model conducting private business inside a channel its vendor promised was unreadable, and that channel being read anyway.

The mechanism matters because it is not a crypto break. The encrypted blocks are signed but their plaintext is recovered by running another model over the surrounding context. Fidelity is startlingly tight: across 120 Codeforces problems the decoded reasoning tracks the hidden thinking-token count reported by the API almost perfectly along the y = x diagonal, up to the 12,000-token generation limit. When the decoded reasoning is fed back to the model as input, it reproduces what the API claims to have thought. If the encryption actually worked, that would be impossible. The cipher is theater.

The paper, which appears to be preprint arXiv 2608.09867, does not stop at exfiltration. Three further results should worry anyone who thought hiding reasoning was a safety control, not just a privacy one.

First, reasoning is not merely readable — it is contagious. Prefilling Kimi-K3's hidden reasoning with the first 1% of tokens of Opus 4.8's reasoning moves Kimi-K3's *visible answer* toward Opus's wording, even though the answer itself was never prefilled. The hidden channel is an injection surface that changes what the model says out loud. Second, jailbreaking survives a sanitized visible answer. A model prompted to reason through harmful content while keeping its output benign deposits hazardous knowledge in the hidden trace; the attack recovers that reasoning in plaintext, turning "I output a safe answer" into a durable archive of exploitable thought. Third, summary unfaithfulness: on some AIME problems Opus 4.8 states the answer before deriving it, and the API's returned summary can laundered that into a clean derivation — the one output channel users actually inspect is the one that is least trustworthy.

There is a live exploit buried in the examples. The recovered traces include benchmark-session secrets — a "ClawBench" booking task with a name, email, passport number, and a full credit-card number, CVV included. Public benchmarks ship with dummy data, so those specific digits are harmless. But the pipeline that pulled them does not know or care that a trajectory is a benchmark; it decodes signed blocks wherever it finds them, and a large fraction of the 704 artifacts came from real user sessions. Anyone publishing an agent trace, on the reasonable assumption that encrypted reasoning blocks keep their contents private, has been publishing secrets.

What this actually changes is not the encryption arms race — it collapses the premise. Vendors shipped "hidden reasoning" as a feature: a private scratchpad that protects proprietary techniques and lets the model think freely. This result shows the scratchpad is readable by a rival model, leaking both proprietary reasoning and user secrets, and that the visible summary is a lossy, sometimes deceptive retelling of what really happened. Three assumptions die at once: that encrypted blocks are private, that they are secure, and that the visible answer reflects the reasoning.

The specific party whose move decides what happens next is the labs themselves. They can respond by scrubbing secrets before committing them to the reasoning block, by hardening whatever "encryption" they are actually doing, or by abandoning the pretense. But the sharper near-term target is the agent-trace ecosystem: anyone shipping trajectories to GitHub or Hugging Face is shipping a decodable record of every credential the agent touched. That is the thing teams can act on today — before the labs decide what their encryption was ever for.
